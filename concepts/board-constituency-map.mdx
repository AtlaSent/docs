## Boards stopped asking how much AI you are deploying.

The question now put to executives is different:

> How do we create enterprise value from AI while protecting customers,
> employees, intellectual property, and shareholder interests?

That question has a concrete answer, and it is not a maturity score. It is an
inventory: **the consequential actions your organization will not let anything
execute without authorization**, grouped by who each action protects.

## The constraint is governance capacity, not model capability

Organizations cap what they delegate to autonomous systems because the blast
radius of a wrong action is unbounded — not because the models cannot do the
work. Every action on this page is one you would otherwise have to keep a human
in front of, and often not for the judgement. For the accountability.

[Authorization at the execution boundary](/concepts/what-can-atlasent-authorize)
raises that cap. The organization delegates the work and keeps the authority.
For a board, that reframes the whole conversation: protection is not the tax on
velocity, it is the precondition for it.

## One action restates the board's old question

<Note>
  `agent.tool.invoke` (`ACT-0029`) governs the moment an autonomous agent
  reaches for a tool. Because it is a protected action like any other, "how much
  AI are we deploying?" stops being a number nobody can source and becomes a
  governed count: how many agent invocations were authorized, under whose
  authority, against which of the four constituencies.

  It is also the only action that can land in all four at once — an agent
  holding a tool can touch a customer record, an employee's access, a release
  pipeline, and a payment. That makes it the first action to instrument, and the
  one whose coverage figure a board should ask for by name.
</Note>

## What this map establishes — and what it does not

The distinction matters more than the inventory. A register that overstates
itself is worth less than no register, because a reader who finds one soft claim
stops trusting the hard ones.

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="What it establishes" icon="circle-check">
    **The governable surface.** These are the canonical action records, each
    with its own risk posture, AI-risk rating, and regulatory mappings.

    **Coverage of all four constituencies.** Every group a board names has
    actions defined against it — not one corner of the estate.

    **A shared vocabulary.** Board, engineering, and audit can name the same
    action with the same identifier.
  </Card>
  <Card title="What it does not establish" icon="circle-minus">
    **That your organization has provisioned these.** A canonical action
    existing is not the same as your policy carrying that action's
    requirements.

    **That the grouping is authoritative.** Assignment to one constituency is a
    presentation choice; the canonical action catalog is the authority.

    **That coverage equals enforcement.** What you actually enforce is a
    separate, evidenced question — asked per action, and per execution path.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

<Warning>
  Do not present this map to a board as deployed coverage. It describes what
  AtlaSent can govern, not what any given organization has switched on. Your
  real coverage figure comes from your own console, against your own
  provisioned action classes — and the gap between the two is the honest
  starting point for a board conversation, not something to paper over.
</Warning>

## The part a dashboard cannot supply

Every vendor in this market can show a director a green screen. The harder
question is whether the director has to believe it.

An authorization record that is hash-linked, Ed25519-signed, and
verifiable offline by a third party — without a
network call, and without trusting the vendor that produced it — is the form of
this claim that survives an adversarial audit. That, not the inventory above, is
what turns governance discipline into a durable advantage.
